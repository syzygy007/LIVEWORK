Interview preparation

Four things,
and they go
in order.

Know your story. Know your numbers. Show respect by listening. Leave one idea behind. Everything else people tell you about interviews is decoration on those four.

Before you start

Everything you write stays in this browser, on this device. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is sent to a recruiter, and nothing is sent to a hiring manager. There is no account and no login, so there is nobody to share it with unless you copy it and send it yourself.

Which means you can be honest in here. The parts you would not say out loud yet are exactly the parts worth writing down.

About 60 minutes if you do all four.
One of four, about 15 minutes

Your story is
not your
resume out loud.

They have already read the resume. The only new information in the room is the part that is not on it: what you noticed, what you decided, what you got wrong and what changed your mind.

A story is not a chronology. It is a thread, and the thread is how you make decisions, because that is what they are actually buying. They are not hiring what you did. They are hiring what you will do in a situation nobody has described yet.

Now say it in thirty seconds.

Pull the four answers above into one spoken paragraph. Thirty seconds is roughly 75 to 90 words. Write it the way you talk, contractions and all.

Two of four, about 20 minutes

Know where
you moved
the needle.

Your responsibilities describe the work you were handed. Your contribution is what got better because you were there. Almost nobody can tell those apart out loud, and the ones who can get hired.

First, three things people get wrong.

Every one of these has cost somebody an offer, because the interviewer knew and the candidate did not.

Now build three records.

One is not enough, because the second question is always another example. Three is the number that makes you look like a pattern instead of an accident. Fill in what you can; the empty boxes are the interview.

Three of four, about 12 minutes

Respect is
demonstrated,
never announced.

Telling someone you could learn a lot from them is a claim about a person you met forty minutes ago, and every manager has heard it. Asking them a question only someone who was listening could ask does the same job and cannot be faked.

Being listened to closely is rarer than being admired. That is the whole mechanism. You are not flattering them, you are paying attention to them, and they will feel the difference without being able to name it.

Three questions you actually want answered.

Test for each one: could you ask it of any company in the country? If yes, it is filler and they know it. Aim it at this role, this team, this quarter.

The follow up is the whole thing.

Anyone can ask a prepared question. The move nobody prepares for is the second one, built out of the words they just used. Try it.

Four of four, about 15 minutes

Leave one
idea behind,
for free.

This is the part that puts you in the room already working there. Not a plan, not a critique of a business you have never seen from the inside. One thing you would want to look at first, and how you would find out whether you were right.

The trap is confidence. Walking in and diagnosing their operation from the outside reads as arrogance to the person who has been living in it for six years. The version that lands is the one that shows your method while admitting you do not have their data yet.

One last thing. The four are in order for a reason. The story earns their attention, the numbers earn their trust, the listening makes them comfortable, and the idea makes them picture you there. Skip the first and the rest has nothing to stand on.
